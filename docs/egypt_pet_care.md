# PetPluse — Egypt Pet Care Knowledge Base

General, non-diagnostic pet-care guidance tailored to Egypt's climate and common conditions. This is general information only and never replaces a licensed veterinarian.

## Vaccination schedule for puppies and dogs
Puppies typically start core vaccinations at 6–8 weeks, with boosters every 3–4 weeks until about 16 weeks of age. Core canine vaccines include distemper, parvovirus, adenovirus, and rabies. In Egypt, rabies is endemic and the rabies vaccine is especially important — it is usually given from 12 weeks of age and boosted yearly. Adult dogs generally need annual or triennial boosters depending on the vaccine. Keep a record of each shot and its due date. Consult your veterinarian for a schedule specific to your dog.

## Vaccination schedule for kittens and cats
Kittens usually begin vaccinations at 6–8 weeks with boosters every 3–4 weeks until about 16 weeks. Core feline vaccines protect against feline panleukopenia, feline herpesvirus, and calicivirus, plus rabies (important in Egypt). Adult cats need regular boosters as advised by a vet. Indoor cats still need core vaccines. Ask your veterinarian which schedule fits your cat.

## Deworming schedule for dogs and cats
Puppies and kittens should be dewormed every 2 weeks from about 2 weeks of age until 12 weeks, then monthly until 6 months old. Adult dogs and cats are usually dewormed every 3 months (quarterly), or more often if they roam outdoors or hunt. Intestinal worms are common in Egypt, especially in street-rescued animals. Signs can include a pot-bellied appearance, weight loss, or worms in the stool. Use a vet-recommended dewormer and confirm dosing with your veterinarian.

## Flea control in a hot climate
Fleas thrive year-round in Egypt's warm climate. Signs include scratching, biting at the skin, tiny dark "flea dirt" in the coat, and hair loss. Control involves treating the pet with a vet-approved flea preventative (spot-on, oral, or collar) and treating the home environment — wash bedding, vacuum regularly, since most of a flea population lives in the environment, not on the pet. Treat all pets in the household. Ask your vet for a safe product for your pet's species, age, and weight — never use dog products on cats.

## Ticks and tick-borne disease
The brown dog tick is common in Egypt and can transmit diseases such as ehrlichiosis and babesiosis. Check your dog's skin, ears, and between the toes after outdoor walks. Remove ticks promptly with a tick tool, grasping close to the skin. Use a vet-recommended tick preventative during warm months (most of the year in Egypt). If your pet becomes lethargic, feverish, or stops eating after tick exposure, see a veterinarian.

## Heatstroke and summer heat care
Egypt's summers are dangerously hot for pets. Heatstroke is an emergency — signs include heavy panting, drooling, bright red gums, weakness, vomiting, or collapse. Never leave a pet in a parked car. Walk dogs in the early morning or late evening, avoid hot pavement (it burns paws), always provide shade and fresh water, and never over-exercise brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds in the heat. If you suspect heatstroke, move the pet to shade, offer water, cool with room-temperature (not ice) water, and get to a vet immediately.

## Feeding puppies
Young puppies need frequent meals: about 4 meals a day up to 3 months, 3 meals from 3–6 months, then 2 meals a day. Feed a quality puppy-formulated food appropriate to their expected adult size. Fresh water must always be available. Avoid cow's milk (it commonly causes diarrhea) and never feed toxic human foods. Ask your vet about portion sizes for your puppy's breed and weight.

## Feeding adult dogs
Most adult dogs do well on two measured meals a day of a complete, balanced food. Portion to maintain a healthy weight — you should be able to feel the ribs without a thick fat layer. Obesity is common and shortens lifespan. Keep fresh water available at all times, especially in Egypt's heat. Avoid sudden diet changes (transition over a week) to prevent stomach upset.

## Feeding cats
Cats are obligate carnivores and need a meat-based, complete cat food. Many cats drink little water, so incorporating wet food helps hydration and urinary health. Feed measured portions to prevent obesity. Do not feed dog food long-term (it lacks taurine, essential for cats). Fresh water should always be available.

## Foods toxic to dogs and cats
Never feed: chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, xylitol (a sweetener in sugar-free gum), macadamia nuts, alcohol, caffeine, or raw dough. Onions and garlic are common in Egyptian cooking, so keep table scraps away from pets. If your pet eats something toxic, treat it as an emergency and contact a vet immediately — do not wait for symptoms.

## Parvovirus in dogs
Parvovirus is a highly contagious, potentially fatal virus, especially dangerous for unvaccinated puppies. Signs include severe vomiting, bloody diarrhea, lethargy, and loss of appetite. It spreads through contaminated feces and environments. Prevention is vaccination on schedule and avoiding public areas until a puppy is fully vaccinated. Suspected parvo is an emergency — see a veterinarian urgently.

## Spaying and neutering
Spaying (females) and neutering (males) prevent unwanted litters, reduce roaming and some behavioral problems, and lower the risk of certain cancers and infections (such as pyometra in females). Timing varies by species, breed, and size — commonly around 6 months, but ask your vet for the right age for your pet. Given the large street-animal population in Egypt, spay/neuter is an important responsible-ownership step.

## Bringing home a street or rescue pet
Newly rescued street animals in Egypt should see a veterinarian early for a health check, including screening for parasites (fleas, ticks, worms), skin conditions like mange, and starting vaccinations and deworming. Keep a new pet separate from resident pets for about a week to watch for illness and reduce disease spread. Provide a quiet space, fresh water, and gentle handling while they adjust.

## Dental care
Dental disease is very common and often overlooked. Signs include bad breath, yellow-brown tartar, red gums, and difficulty eating. Brush your pet's teeth with pet-safe toothpaste (never human toothpaste), offer dental chews, and have a vet check the teeth during routine visits. Untreated dental disease can cause pain and affect other organs.

## Hydration and clean water
Always provide clean, fresh water, refreshed daily — this is critical in Egypt's heat to prevent dehydration and urinary problems. Signs of dehydration include tacky gums, lethargy, and skin that is slow to spring back. Cats in particular benefit from extra water sources and wet food. If a pet stops drinking or you see signs of dehydration, consult a vet.

## When to see a veterinarian
See a vet promptly for: repeated vomiting or diarrhea, not eating for more than a day, difficulty breathing, straining or unable to urinate, blood in urine or stool, seizures, collapse, suspected poisoning, being hit by a car, or heatstroke. When in doubt, it is always safer to have a licensed veterinarian examine your pet. PetPluse and its AI assistant provide general information only and cannot diagnose.
