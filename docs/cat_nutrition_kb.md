# PetPulse — Cat Nutrition & Care Knowledge Base

General, non-diagnostic feline guidance synthesized from trusted veterinary sources
(VCA Animal Hospitals, the AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines, and the ASPCA).
This is general information only and never replaces a licensed veterinarian.

## Feeding an adult cat — diet and nutrition
What should you feed an adult cat, and what nutrition does a cat need to eat to stay healthy? Cats are obligate carnivores: they must get key nutrients from animal-based protein and cannot thrive on a dog's or a vegetarian diet. Choose a complete and balanced cat food that meets AAFCO standards for the adult (maintenance) life stage — protein around 30–40% on a dry-matter basis is typical, above the AAFCO minimum of about 26%. Taurine, an amino acid found only in animal protein, is essential for a cat: a deficiency causes blindness (retinal degeneration) and a serious heart disease (dilated cardiomyopathy), so never feed dog food long-term because it is not formulated with enough taurine for cats. Feed measured portions at least twice a day; many cats do well with three to four small meals, which suits their natural grazing pattern and helps cats that beg or eat little at one sitting. Portion to keep a lean body condition — you should feel the ribs easily with only a thin fat cover — because feline obesity is common and shortens life. Because cats have a low thirst drive, including wet (canned) food supports hydration and urinary-tract health. Always leave fresh, clean water available. Your veterinarian sets the exact daily amount based on your cat's weight, age, neuter status, and body-condition score. (Guidance aligned with VCA, AAHA/AAFP, and ASPCA.)

## How much and how often to feed a cat
Portion-controlled feeding — measuring the daily amount and dividing it into meals — is preferred over leaving food out all day (free feeding), which drives overeating and obesity. Twice daily is a sensible default for most adult cats; three to four small meals can suit grazers or cats on wet food. Wet food should not be left out for long periods because it spoils, so it fits scheduled meals better than free feeding. Use the calorie guide on the food label as a starting point only, then adjust to keep your cat lean, and have your vet confirm the target amount for your individual cat. Treats should make up no more than about 10% of daily calories. (Guidance aligned with VCA and AAHA/AAFP.)

## Feeding kittens
Kittens are growing fast and need a food labelled complete and balanced for growth (kitten) or for all life stages, which is higher in protein, calories, and DHA than adult food. Feed frequent small meals — around four a day for very young kittens, tapering to three, then transitioning to adult feeding by roughly 10–12 months of age (large breeds such as Maine Coons may stay on kitten food a little longer). Avoid cow's milk; despite the stereotype, many cats are lactose intolerant and it causes diarrhea. Provide constant access to fresh water. Confirm the schedule and portions with your veterinarian. (Guidance aligned with VCA and AAHA/AAFP.)

## Cat hydration and urinary health
Cats descend from desert animals and have a naturally low thirst drive, so many drink too little — a risk factor for urinary and kidney problems. Encourage water intake by feeding wet food, offering several clean water bowls around the home, keeping water away from the litter box and food, and trying a pet water fountain since many cats prefer moving water. This matters even more in Egypt's heat. See a vet promptly if your cat strains in the litter box, cries when urinating, passes blood, or (especially in male cats) cannot pass urine — a urinary blockage is a life-threatening emergency. (Guidance aligned with VCA and ASPCA.)

## Feline obesity and weight management
Obesity is one of the most common feline health problems and raises the risk of diabetes, arthritis, and urinary disease. Indoor and neutered cats need fewer calories and are especially prone to weight gain. Use body-condition scoring rather than the bag's generic guide: a healthy cat has an easily felt ribcage, a visible waist from above, and only a small belly fat pad. Manage weight with measured meals, a complete diet, limited treats (under 10% of calories), and play for activity — never crash-diet a cat, because rapid weight loss can trigger a dangerous liver disease (hepatic lipidosis). Ask your vet to set a safe target weight and rate of loss. (Guidance aligned with AAHA/AAFP and VCA.)

## Foods and plants dangerous to cats
Never give a cat: onions, garlic, leeks or chives (they damage red blood cells and cause anemia — a concern with Egyptian table scraps), chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, grapes and raisins, xylitol sweetener, or raw dough. Do not rely on tuna or dog food as a diet — tuna lacks feline nutrients and dog food lacks taurine. Critically for cats, true lilies (Lilium and Hemerocallis) are extremely toxic: even a nibble of leaf or petal, or licking pollen, can cause fatal kidney failure, so keep lilies out of any home with cats. If your cat eats something toxic, treat it as an emergency and contact a veterinarian immediately — do not wait for symptoms. (Guidance aligned with ASPCA Animal Poison Control and VCA.)
