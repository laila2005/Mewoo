# PetPulse — Everyday Pet Care Knowledge Base

General, non-diagnostic guidance on the questions owners ask most, synthesized
from trusted veterinary sources (VCA Animal Hospitals, AAHA, the ASPCA, and AVMA
owner resources) and adapted to Egypt where relevant. General information only —
never a substitute for a licensed veterinarian.

## New puppy checklist for the first weeks
Bringing home a puppy? Book a first vet visit early for a health check, start the vaccination series (usually from 6–8 weeks, with rabies especially important in Egypt), and begin a deworming schedule. Feed a quality puppy-formulated food in several small meals a day, always with fresh water. Start gentle socialization and short positive training sessions, puppy-proof the home (hide wires and toxic plants), and introduce a crate or safe space. Book spay/neuter guidance for the right age. Keep the puppy away from unvaccinated dogs and public areas until fully vaccinated. Ask your veterinarian for a schedule tailored to your puppy. (Aligned with AAHA and VCA.)

## New kitten checklist for the first weeks
For a new kitten, schedule an early vet exam, start core feline vaccinations (from 6–8 weeks) and deworming, and screen for parasites — important for street-rescued kittens common in Egypt. Feed a complete kitten (growth) food in frequent small meals, and provide constant fresh water. Set up a quiet litter box, a scratching post, and safe toys, and let the kitten adjust in one room before exploring. Avoid cow's milk (it commonly causes diarrhea). Discuss microchipping and the right age for spay/neuter. Confirm the plan with your veterinarian. (Aligned with AAHA/AAFP and VCA.)

## Caring for a senior dog
Dogs are generally considered senior around 7 years (later for small breeds, earlier for giant breeds). Senior dogs benefit from twice-yearly vet check-ups, blood work to catch kidney, liver, or thyroid issues early, and dental care. Watch for weight change, stiffness or trouble rising (arthritis is common), cloudy eyes, lumps, increased thirst, or confusion. Keep them at a lean weight, provide soft bedding and gentle regular exercise, and consider a senior diet or joint support as advised. Report new signs promptly rather than dismissing them as "just old age." (Aligned with AAHA senior-care guidelines and VCA.)

## Caring for a senior cat
Cats are considered senior from about 10–11 years. Older cats hide illness well, so schedule twice-yearly vet visits with blood and urine tests — kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, and high blood pressure are common and very treatable when caught early. Watch for weight loss, increased thirst or urination, changes in appetite or grooming, and difficulty jumping. Keep litter boxes easy to reach, feed a good senior diet, encourage water intake, and keep the cat lean and active. Any change in a senior cat deserves a vet check. (Aligned with AAHA/AAFP feline senior-care guidance.)

## Dog vomiting and diarrhea — home care versus the vet
Occasional mild vomiting or soft stool in an otherwise bright, active dog can sometimes settle with 12–24 hours of a bland diet (plain boiled chicken and rice) and fresh water. See a vet promptly — do not wait — if there is repeated vomiting, blood in vomit or stool, a bloated or painful belly, weakness, no appetite for more than a day, signs of dehydration, or if the dog is a young puppy (parvovirus risk) or may have eaten something toxic or a foreign object. When unsure, it is always safer to have a vet examine the dog. (Aligned with VCA and AVMA. This is not a diagnosis.)

## Cat vomiting and hairballs
An occasional hairball is common, especially in long-haired cats, and regular brushing plus a good diet helps reduce them. However, frequent vomiting is NOT normal and should not be dismissed as "just hairballs." See a vet if your cat vomits repeatedly, vomits and cannot keep water down, loses weight or appetite, seems lethargic, or strains without producing a hairball. Persistent vomiting can signal dietary issues, parasites, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, or a blockage. When in doubt, have a veterinarian check your cat. (Aligned with VCA and AAFP. Not a diagnosis.)

## Itching, skin, and ear problems
Constant scratching, licking, chewing, hair loss, scabs, redness, head-shaking, or smelly ears usually mean something needs attention. Common causes include fleas (very common year-round in Egypt), environmental or food allergies, mites (mange), and ear infections. Keep up flea prevention, avoid over-bathing, and never put anything in the ear canal without vet advice. Because many skin and ear conditions look alike but need different treatment, a vet should examine the pet rather than guessing at an over-the-counter product. (Aligned with VCA and ASPCA. Not a diagnosis.)

## Shedding and grooming
Regular brushing removes loose hair, reduces shedding and hairballs, and lets you spot lumps, fleas, or skin problems early — daily for long coats, weekly for short. Bathe only as needed with a pet-appropriate shampoo (human shampoo irritates pet skin). Keep nails trimmed, check ears, and brush teeth with pet-safe toothpaste. Sudden heavy shedding, bald patches, or a dull, greasy coat can indicate parasites, allergies, stress, or an underlying illness — worth a vet check. (Aligned with VCA and AAHA.)

## House-training a puppy
House-training works fastest with routine and positive reinforcement. Take the puppy out first thing in the morning, after meals, after naps, after play, and before bed, and reward immediately outdoors with praise or a treat. Supervise closely indoors or use a crate sized so the puppy won't soil it. Clean accidents with an enzymatic cleaner so odors don't draw them back, and never punish accidents — it teaches fear, not cleanliness. Most puppies gain reliable control by 4–6 months as their bladder matures. (Aligned with AVMA and ASPCA behavior guidance.)

## Litter box basics for cats
Cats are naturally clean and usually use a litter box readily if it's set up well. Provide one box per cat plus one extra, in quiet, easy-to-reach spots away from food and water. Scoop daily and wash the box regularly; many cats dislike scented litter or covered boxes. Sudden litter-box avoidance, straining, or urinating outside the box is not spite — it often signals a urinary or medical problem (especially in male cats, a possible blockage is an emergency) and warrants a prompt vet visit. (Aligned with AAFP and ASPCA.)

## Microchipping and pet identification
A microchip is a permanent ID the size of a rice grain, placed under the skin by quick injection, that links your pet to your contact details in a registry — the best way to be reunited if they are lost. It does not track location like GPS; it is read by a scanner at a vet or shelter. Keep your registry details up to date, and pair the chip with a collar and visible ID tag. Microchipping is quick, low-cost, and recommended for both dogs and cats. Ask your veterinarian about chipping and registration. (Aligned with AVMA and AAHA.)

## Pet anxiety and behavior basics
Signs of stress or anxiety include hiding, pacing, excessive barking or meowing, destructive chewing, trembling, or house-soiling — often triggered by loud noises, separation, or changes at home. Help by keeping predictable routines, providing a safe quiet space, enrichment (toys, play, scratching posts), and gradual positive exposure to triggers; never punish fearful behavior. For sudden or severe changes, rule out pain or illness with a vet first, since discomfort often shows up as behavior change. A vet or qualified behaviorist can guide a plan for persistent anxiety. (Aligned with AVMA and ASPCA behavior guidance.)

## Exercise and enrichment for dogs
Most dogs need daily physical exercise and mental stimulation to stay healthy and well-behaved — under-exercised dogs often develop barking, chewing, or restlessness. Amount varies by age, breed, and health: many adult dogs do well with 30–60+ minutes a day split into walks and play, while puppies and seniors need gentler, shorter sessions. In Egypt's heat, walk in the early morning or late evening, avoid hot pavement, and always bring water. Add mental enrichment with training games, sniff walks, and food puzzles. Ask your vet what's right for your dog's age and condition. (Aligned with AVMA and AAHA.)

## Minor first aid basics (non-emergency)
For a small, clean scrape, you can gently rinse it with clean water and keep the pet from licking it; monitor for swelling, discharge, or worsening and see a vet if it doesn't improve. Do NOT give human pain medicines (ibuprofen, paracetamol/acetaminophen, aspirin) — they are toxic to dogs and cats. Keep a basic kit: gauze, non-stick pads, vet-approved antiseptic, tweezers, and your vet's number. Always treat as an emergency and go to a vet for heavy bleeding, difficulty breathing, suspected poisoning, seizures, being hit by a car, a bloated belly, or inability to urinate. (Aligned with AVMA and VCA. Not a diagnosis.)
