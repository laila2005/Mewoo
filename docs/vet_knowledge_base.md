# Veterinary Knowledge Base

This document serves as a comprehensive reference guide for common pet health conditions, preventive care, emergencies, and general wellness. It is designed to provide medically accurate information in accessible language for pet owners.

## 1. Common Dog Diseases

### Canine Parvovirus
**Description**: A highly contagious viral illness that primarily attacks the gastrointestinal tract of dogs. It is most severe in puppies.
**Common symptoms**: Severe, bloody diarrhea, lethargy, anorexia, fever, vomiting, and severe weight loss.
**When to see a vet**: IMMEDIATELY if you suspect parvovirus, especially in puppies. It is rapidly fatal without intensive veterinary treatment.
**Prevention tips**: Ensure puppies receive their full series of parvovirus vaccinations starting at 6-8 weeks of age.

### Canine Distemper
**Description**: A contagious and serious disease caused by a virus that attacks the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and nervous systems of puppies and dogs.
**Common symptoms**: Watery to pus-like discharge from eyes, fever, nasal discharge, coughing, lethargy, reduced appetite, and vomiting. Muscle twitches and seizures can occur in later stages.
**When to see a vet**: Immediately upon noticing symptoms, as it is often fatal and highly contagious.
**Prevention tips**: Vaccination is highly effective and essential for all dogs.

### Kennel Cough (Bordetella)
**Description**: A highly contagious respiratory disease. Dogs commonly contract it at places where large amounts of canines congregate, such as boarding and daycare facilities.
**Common symptoms**: A strong cough, often with a "honking" sound, runny nose, sneezing, lethargy, loss of appetite, and a low fever.
**When to see a vet**: If the cough persists for more than a few days, or if the dog is a puppy, senior, or immunocompromised.
**Prevention tips**: The Bordetella vaccine is recommended for dogs who frequently interact with other dogs.

### Hip Dysplasia
**Description**: A skeletal condition where the ball and socket of the hip joint do not fit or develop properly, rubbing and grinding instead of sliding smoothly.
**Common symptoms**: Decreased activity, decreased range of motion, difficulty or reluctance rising, jumping, running, or climbing stairs, lameness in the hind end, swaying, "bunny hopping" gait.
**When to see a vet**: When you notice signs of pain or difficulty moving.
**Prevention tips**: Maintain a healthy weight, provide appropriate exercise, and consider joint supplements (under vet guidance).

### Heartworm Disease
**Description**: A serious and potentially fatal disease caused by foot-long worms (heartworms) that live in the heart, lungs and associated blood vessels, transmitted by mosquitoes.
**Common symptoms**: Mild persistent cough, reluctance to exercise, fatigue after moderate activity, decreased appetite, and weight loss.
**When to see a vet**: Annual testing is recommended; see a vet immediately if symptoms appear.
**Prevention tips**: Year-round administration of heartworm prevention medication.

### Lyme Disease
**Description**: A tick-borne illness caused by the bacterium *Borrelia burgdorferi*.
**Common symptoms**: Fever, loss of appetite, reduced energy, lameness (can be shifting, intermittent, and recurring), generalized stiffness, discomfort, or pain.
**When to see a vet**: If you notice lameness or fever, especially if your dog has been exposed to ticks.
**Prevention tips**: Use year-round tick preventatives, check for ticks daily, and consider the Lyme vaccine for dogs in high-risk areas.

### Rabies (Canine)
**Description**: A fatal viral disease that affects the central nervous system of mammals.
**Common symptoms**: Behavioral changes, unprovoked aggression, paralysis, difficulty swallowing, drooling, seizures.
**When to see a vet**: Any potential exposure to wildlife requires immediate veterinary attention. Once symptoms appear, it is fatal.
**Prevention tips**: Vaccination is legally required in most areas and is 100% effective when kept up to date.

### Gastric Dilatation-Volvulus (Bloat)
**Description**: A rapidly progressive life-threatening condition where the stomach fills with gas and twists on itself.
**Common symptoms**: Unproductive vomiting or retching, swollen/distended abdomen, restlessness, drooling, panting, pacing.
**When to see a vet**: THIS IS A CRITICAL EMERGENCY. Go to the nearest vet immediately.
**Prevention tips**: Feed smaller, more frequent meals, avoid exercise immediately after eating, and consider prophylactic gastropexy for high-risk breeds.

### Canine Pancreatitis
**Description**: Inflammation of the pancreas, which can be acute or chronic, often triggered by eating a high-fat meal.
**Common symptoms**: Vomiting, abdominal pain (hunched back), diarrhea, loss of appetite, lethargy, fever.
**When to see a vet**: Promptly, as severe acute pancreatitis can be life-threatening.
**Prevention tips**: Avoid feeding high-fat human foods (like table scraps) and maintain your dog at a healthy weight.

### Canine Allergies
**Description**: An overactive immune response to everyday substances (allergens) like pollen, dust mites, mold, or certain food ingredients.
**Common symptoms**: Itchy skin, frequent scratching, licking paws, red or inflamed skin, ear infections, gastrointestinal issues (if a food allergy).
**When to see a vet**: When itching becomes disruptive to the dog's life, causes skin infections, or if there are chronic ear infections.
**Prevention tips**: Identify and avoid allergens where possible; use vet-prescribed allergy management medications.

## 2. Common Cat Diseases

### Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)
**Description**: A viral infection that attacks a cat's immune system, leaving the cat vulnerable to other infections. It is typically spread through deep bite wounds.
**Common symptoms**: Often asymptomatic for years. Later symptoms include poor coat condition, persistent fever, loss of appetite, gingivitis, chronic or recurrent infections.
**When to see a vet**: See a vet for testing if a cat has been in a fight, or if experiencing recurrent illnesses.
**Prevention tips**: Keep cats indoors to prevent fights with infected cats.

### Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV)
**Description**: A retrovirus that impairs the cat's immune system and can cause certain types of cancer. It is spread through saliva, nasal secretions, urine, feces, and milk of infected cats.
**Common symptoms**: Loss of appetite, weight loss, poor coat condition, enlarged lymph nodes, persistent fever, pale gums, gingivitis.
**When to see a vet**: Regular testing is recommended; see a vet if any chronic symptoms arise.
**Prevention tips**: Keep cats indoors, test new cats before introducing them, and vaccinate at-risk cats.

### Upper Respiratory Infections (URI)
**Description**: Complex of respiratory infections caused by viruses and bacteria (e.g., Feline Herpesvirus, Feline Calicivirus).
**Common symptoms**: Sneezing, nasal congestion, runny nose, coughing, clear to purulent discharge from eyes/nose, fever, loss of appetite.
**When to see a vet**: If the cat stops eating, has trouble breathing, or symptoms persist for more than a few days.
**Prevention tips**: Core vaccinations provide significant protection against the most severe viruses causing URIs.

### Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
**Description**: A progressive and irreversible loss of kidney function over time, very common in older cats.
**Common symptoms**: Increased thirst, increased urination, weight loss, decreased appetite, lethargy, vomiting.
**When to see a vet**: As soon as you notice changes in thirst or urination habits.
**Prevention tips**: Ensure adequate water intake throughout life; regular senior health screenings can catch it early for better management.

### Feline Diabetes
**Description**: A condition where a cat's body cannot effectively regulate blood sugar levels, commonly Type II (insulin resistance).
**Common symptoms**: Increased thirst, increased urination, weight loss despite a good appetite, lethargy.
**When to see a vet**: Promptly upon noticing increased thirst and urination.
**Prevention tips**: Keep cats at a lean, healthy weight and feed an appropriate diet (high protein, low carbohydrate).

### Hyperthyroidism
**Description**: A glandular disorder where the thyroid gland produces too much thyroid hormone, increasing the metabolic rate.
**Common symptoms**: Weight loss despite increased appetite, increased thirst and urination, vomiting, diarrhea, hyperactivity, vocalizing more than usual.
**When to see a vet**: If you notice weight loss with an increased appetite in an older cat.
**Prevention tips**: There is no proven prevention; regular blood tests for senior cats help with early detection.

### Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease (FLUTD/UTIs)
**Description**: A broad term describing conditions that can affect the urinary bladder and urethra of cats.
**Common symptoms**: Straining to urinate, urinating small amounts frequently, blood in the urine, crying while urinating, urinating outside the litter box.
**When to see a vet**: IMMEDIATELY if the cat is straining but unable to pass urine (this is a fatal emergency). Otherwise, promptly for any signs of discomfort.
**Prevention tips**: Ensure a clean litter box, promote water intake (e.g., water fountains, wet food), and minimize stress.

### Dental Disease (Periodontal Disease)
**Description**: Infection and inflammation of the gums and supporting structures of the teeth.
**Common symptoms**: Bad breath, difficulty chewing, drooling, pawing at the mouth, bleeding gums, tooth loss.
**When to see a vet**: For regular checkups and if you notice any oral pain or bad breath.
**Prevention tips**: Daily tooth brushing (with pet-safe toothpaste), dental diets, and regular professional cleanings.

### Feline Asthma
**Description**: An inflammatory disease of the lower airways in the lungs, causing constriction and difficulty breathing.
**Common symptoms**: Coughing, wheezing, rapid breathing, open-mouth breathing, vomiting up "hairballs" that are actually coughs.
**When to see a vet**: Promptly for diagnosis. Open-mouth breathing is an emergency.
**Prevention tips**: Minimize airborne irritants in the home (smoke, strong perfumes, dusty cat litter, aerosols).

### Ringworm (Dermatophytosis)
**Description**: A highly contagious fungal infection of the skin, hair, and nails (not a worm).
**Common symptoms**: Circular patches of hair loss, scaling, crusty skin, and red lesions, often on the head, ears, or forelimbs.
**When to see a vet**: If you notice unusual hair loss or skin lesions. Note: Ringworm is zoonotic (can spread to humans).
**Prevention tips**: Avoid contact with infected animals and decontaminate the environment if an outbreak occurs.

## 3. Preventive Care

### Puppy & Kitten Vaccination Schedules
**Description**: Core vaccinations are essential for building a young animal's immune system against deadly diseases.
**Guidelines**:
- Puppies (Core: Parvovirus, Distemper, Adenovirus, Rabies): Series begins at 6-8 weeks, repeating every 3-4 weeks until 16 weeks of age. Rabies is given once at 12-16 weeks.
- Kittens (Core: Panleukopenia, Herpesvirus, Calicivirus, Rabies): Series begins at 6-8 weeks, repeating every 3-4 weeks until 16 weeks of age. Rabies is given once at 12-16 weeks.

### Adult Pet Vaccination Schedules
**Description**: Booster shots to maintain immunity throughout adulthood.
**Guidelines**: Core vaccines are typically boosted at one year of age, and then every 1 to 3 years depending on the vaccine type and local regulations (especially for Rabies). Non-core vaccines (like Bordetella, Lyme, FeLV) are based on lifestyle risk.

### Flea & Tick Prevention
**Description**: Preventing external parasites that cause discomfort, allergies, and transmit serious diseases.
**Guidelines**: Administer year-round, vet-approved preventive medications (oral, topical, or collar). Over-the-counter products are often less effective and sometimes toxic, especially to cats.

### Routine Dental Care
**Description**: Maintaining oral hygiene to prevent periodontal disease, which can impact systemic health (heart, liver, kidneys).
**Guidelines**: Daily brushing with pet-specific toothpaste is the gold standard. Supplemental care includes dental treats, water additives, and annual veterinary examinations/professional cleanings under anesthesia.

### General Exercise Recommendations
**Description**: Physical activity necessary for weight management, cardiovascular health, and mental stimulation.
**Guidelines**:
- Dogs: 30 minutes to 2 hours daily, depending on breed, age, and health. Includes walks, running, and play.
- Cats: At least two 10-15 minute sessions of interactive play daily (e.g., using wand toys, laser pointers) to simulate hunting behaviors.

## 4. Emergency Signs

### Gastric Dilatation-Volvulus (Bloat)
**Condition**: The stomach fills with gas and twists.
**Signs**: Unproductive retching, distended/hard abdomen, extreme restlessness, drooling, pale gums.
**Action**: IMMEDIATE veterinary emergency. Minutes matter.

### Respiratory Distress (Difficulty Breathing)
**Condition**: Inability to get enough oxygen.
**Signs**: Fast breathing, open-mouth breathing (especially in cats), exaggerated abdominal movement while breathing, blue or pale gums, extending the neck to breathe.
**Action**: IMMEDIATE veterinary emergency. Keep the pet calm.

### Neurological Emergencies (Seizures)
**Condition**: Abnormal electrical activity in the brain.
**Signs**: Loss of consciousness, muscle twitching, paddling legs, foaming at the mouth, loss of bowel/bladder control.
**Action**: Keep hands away from their mouth, clear the area of hazards. If the seizure lasts more than 3-5 minutes, or multiple occur in a row, rush to the vet.

### Poisoning and Toxin Ingestion
**Condition**: Ingestion of substances toxic to pets.
**Signs**: Vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, seizures, bleeding, abnormal heart rate, collapsing.
**Action**: Call a vet or poison control hotline immediately. Do NOT induce vomiting unless instructed by a professional.

### Major Trauma
**Condition**: Severe physical injury (e.g., hit by a car, animal attack, falling from a height).
**Signs**: Bleeding, limping, inability to stand, pale gums, altered mental state, apparent pain.
**Action**: Transport to a vet immediately. Handle carefully to avoid being bitten by a pet in pain.

## 5. Pet First Aid

### Minor Cuts and Scrapes
**Action**: Gently clean the area with warm water or a dilute chlorhexidine solution. Do not use hydrogen peroxide or alcohol, as they damage tissue. Apply a pet-safe antibiotic ointment and prevent the pet from licking the wound (use a cone/E-collar if necessary).

### Burns
**Action**: Flush the burn with cool water for several minutes. Do not apply ice, butter, or human burn creams. Cover with a clean, dry, non-stick bandage and seek veterinary care, as burns are very prone to infection.

### Choking
**Action**: Carefully check the mouth to see if the object is easily visible and removable. Do not push it further down. If you cannot remove it and the pet is unconscious, you may need to perform a modified Heimlich maneuver for pets. Seek immediate veterinary help.

### Heatstroke
**Action**: Remove the pet from the hot environment immediately. Apply cool (not ice cold) water to their belly, paws, and ears. Offer small amounts of water to drink. Transport to a vet immediately, as heatstroke can cause delayed organ failure.

### Insect Stings
**Action**: Check for a stinger and scrape it out with a credit card if visible (do not squeeze it). Apply a cold compress. Monitor for allergic reactions (facial swelling, hives, difficulty breathing). Contact a vet; they may recommend a specific dose of Benadryl.

## 6. Nutrition

### Diet Types
- **Kibble (Dry Food)**: Convenient, long shelf-life, can help scrape plaque off teeth. Ensure it meets AAFCO standards.
- **Wet Food (Canned)**: High moisture content (excellent for cats and kidney health), highly palatable, good for pets with dental issues.
- **Raw Diet**: Involves uncooked meat, bones, and organs. Requires careful balancing and strict hygiene to prevent bacterial contamination (e.g., Salmonella, E. coli). Consult a veterinary nutritionist before starting.

### Highly Toxic Foods for Pets
- **Chocolate & Cocoa**: Contains theobromine; causes vomiting, diarrhea, rapid heart rate, tremors, seizures, and death.
- **Grapes & Raisins**: Causes sudden kidney failure in dogs.
- **Onions, Garlic, Chives**: Destroys red blood cells, leading to severe anemia.
- **Xylitol (Artificial Sweetener)**: Found in sugar-free gum, peanut butter, and baked goods. Causes a massive insulin release leading to severe hypoglycemia and acute liver failure.

### Breed & Size-Specific Nutrition
**Guidelines**: Large breed puppies require diets carefully balanced in calcium and phosphorus to prevent developmental orthopedic diseases (like hip dysplasia). Small breeds often need kibble appropriate for their jaw size and higher calorie density.

### Weight Management
**Guidelines**: Obesity is an epidemic in pets, leading to diabetes, joint disease, and reduced lifespan. Measure food using a standard measuring cup, limit treats to 10% of daily caloric intake, and adjust portions based on the pet's body condition score, not just the bag guidelines.

## 7. Behavioral Health

### Separation Anxiety
**Description**: Distress exhibited by a pet when separated from their owner.
**Signs**: Destructive behavior (especially at doors/windows), continuous vocalization, inappropriate elimination, drooling, pacing.
**Management**: Desensitization training, creating a calm environment (music, pheromones), enrichment toys (stuffed KONGs), and in severe cases, veterinary prescribed anti-anxiety medications.

### Aggression
**Description**: Hostile, injurious, or destructive behavior. Can be fear-based, territorial, resource guarding, or pain-induced.
**Signs**: Growling, showing teeth, lunging, biting, stiff body posture.
**Management**: First, rule out underlying medical issues (pain). Then, consult a certified professional dog trainer or veterinary behaviorist. Do not use punishment, which can worsen fear-based aggression.

### Feline Litter Box Issues
**Description**: Urinating or defecating outside the provided litter box.
**Signs**: Finding waste on carpets, beds, or corners.
**Management**: Rule out medical issues (UTI, crystals) first. Ensure the "N+1 rule" (one litter box per cat, plus one extra). Scoop daily, avoid scented litters, place boxes in quiet, accessible areas, and do not use covered boxes if the cat dislikes them.

### Destructive Chewing
**Description**: Chewing on furniture, shoes, or household items, common in puppies but can occur in adult dogs.
**Signs**: Damaged household items.
**Management**: Provide plenty of appropriate, durable chew toys. Manage the environment (crate training, puppy-proofing). Ensure the dog is receiving adequate physical and mental exercise to prevent boredom.

### Socialization
**Description**: The process of exposing a young pet to a variety of people, animals, environments, and stimuli in a positive way.
**Guidelines**: Critical period for puppies is 3-14 weeks; for kittens, it's 2-7 weeks. Expose them gently to new sights, sounds, surfaces, and handling to prevent fearful behavior later in life.

## 8. Senior Pet Care

### Age-Related Conditions
**Description**: As pets age, their organ functions decline and wear-and-tear diseases appear.
**Common Conditions**: Arthritis, chronic kidney disease (especially cats), dental disease, heart disease, vision/hearing loss, and cancer.
**Care**: Biannual senior wellness exams with bloodwork and urinalysis to catch diseases early.

### Mobility Support
**Description**: Assisting pets with osteoarthritis or muscle loss.
**Care**: Provide orthopaedic beds, use rugs or yoga mats on slippery floors, use ramps for stairs or getting into cars, keep nails trimmed short, and discuss pain management (NSAIDs, joint supplements, physical therapy) with a vet.

### Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome (CDS)
**Description**: The canine/feline equivalent of Alzheimer's disease.
**Signs**: Disorientation, getting trapped in corners, changes in sleep-wake cycle, house soiling, changes in interactions with owners.
**Care**: Provide mental stimulation (puzzle toys), stick to a predictable routine, avoid moving furniture, and discuss diets and medications for brain health with a vet.

### End-of-Life Care (Hospice and Euthanasia)
**Description**: Managing a pet's final days with a focus on quality of life and comfort.
**Considerations**: Monitor the "HHHHHMM Quality of Life Scale" (Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility, More good days than bad). When bad days outnumber the good, humane euthanasia, sometimes performed at home, is a compassionate choice to prevent suffering.
